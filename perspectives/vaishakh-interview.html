What owner alignment actually looks like in practice

Veritas Perspectives · Published 1 October 2026 · Updated 3 October 2026

Vaishakh Surendran  ·  Mercure Chennai, Sriperumbudur

← Back to Perspectives

Most GMs talk about owner alignment. Fewer have thought through what it actually requires at the operational level -- the daily decisions that either protect a long-term asset or quietly erode it.

In this interview, Vaishakh Surendran reflects on his time as General Manager of Mercure Chennai (Sriperumbudur), a 100-room Accor property in one of the city's main industrial corridors. The property serves a demanding and unusually diverse guest profile: manufacturing executives from Japan, China, Korea, Taiwan, Malaysia, and Europe, alongside Indian corporate travellers and technical staff from roughly 400 companies in the surrounding industrial zone.

What emerges is a clear-eyed account of how genuine owner orientation shapes everything from pricing strategy to breakfast menus.

The 20-year hotel

The clearest statement of Vaishakh's philosophy comes near the end of the interview, discussing rate strategy in a cost-sensitive industrial market:

"My owners build a hotel to sustain for at least 10 to 20 years, not for 5 years. If I keep on increasing rates every year it will not help me. My long-term objective is to make sure that my hotel is sustainable -- that it offers value for money for the customer as well as for us. It has to be win-win."

This is not a modest aspiration. It means resisting the short-term yield pressure that most operators face from brand benchmarking systems, and instead building durable corporate account relationships that survive rate sensitivity. His response to the ceiling was not to push through it but to create value-added room categories -- Bluetooth speakers, complimentary minibar, laundry, airport transfers -- that justify a modest premium without alienating the account base.

Adapting to the client, not the other way around

One of the more instructive passages in the interview describes a billing problem specific to the industrial corridor context. Corporate clients in manufacturing operate through rigid purchase order systems: a PO for 20 guests means payment for 20 guests, regardless of who signed the event bill. A discrepancy of even one person can block payment indefinitely across multiple approval layers in different departments and locations.

Rather than treating this as a client error to be corrected, Vaishakh's team restructured their own processes to match the procurement system -- becoming, in his words, the client's "preferred vendor" rather than expecting 400 companies to adapt to standard hospitality billing practices. The lesson is simple and often ignored: the market does not conform to your operations model.

Guest strategy across cultures

The same principle applies to the food and beverage programme. The hotel's corporate base includes guests from East and Southeast Asia who had been underserved by a standard South Indian and Continental breakfast. Rather than offering a compromise menu, the team researched shared ingredients across Indian and Asian cuisines -- finding meaningful overlaps in the use of shallots, glass noodles, rice preparations, and specific meat preferences -- and built a dedicated Chinese breakfast offering.

The result: a major corporate account that had been considering alternatives returned, and complaint rates from Asian guests dropped significantly. The insight was not that the hotel needed to serve everything to everyone, but that a modest, well-researched addition could resolve a disproportionate amount of friction.

Sustainability as competitive differentiation

Mercure Chennai was, at the time of filming, the only hotel in its corridor operating a full EV fleet — three BYD and two Tata vehicles covering all airport and office transfers — alongside full elimination of single-use plastics across guest rooms, food and beverage, and back-of-house. The hotel also maintained an organic kitchen garden supplying fresh produce for buffet service, with remaining supply sourced from farms within a 10-kilometre radius.

These were genuine cost commitments in a market where cost control is a primary competitive variable. The decision to carry them reflects a view that sustainable practices — particularly around fleet and waste — become differentiators in the medium term as client procurement teams increasingly weight them in vendor selection.

That view extended to Vaishakh’s subsequent role at Lyfe Hotels & Resorts Bhubaneswar, which in April 2025 was formally registered under the IGBC Green Building Rating System of the Indian Green Building Council — a CII-affiliated body whose certification is now recognised by SEBI disclosure frameworks and an increasing number of corporate procurement policies. On World Earth Day 2025, Lyfe Bhubaneswar received the IGBC Green Crusader designation, placing it among a small group of Indian hotel assets with independently verified sustainable building credentials. The distinction matters less as a badge and more as evidence of the operational discipline required to earn it.

Team as a business strategy

The interview closes on staff culture, and here Vaishakh is unusually specific. The property runs a flat promotion structure in which front-of-house staff progress directly from GSA to Duty Manager, skipping the supervisor and executive levels common in most branded hotels. Starting salaries are uniform across departments. Industrial trainees receive ₹8,000 per month, converting to ₹20,000 at full employment within six months.

The employee engagement score at the time was 8.8 against a brand average that it significantly exceeded. The correlation between that number and the property's RevPAR and guest satisfaction trajectory is not accidental.
