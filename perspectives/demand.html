India's domestic travel boom is not a room-night forecast

Veritas Perspectives · Published 2 October 2026 · Updated 25 October 2026

← Back to Perspectives

Visits do not tell you what your hotel will sell

A tourism headline can make a growth forecast feel safe. Yet your hotel sells rooms on particular dates, not a percentage of national enthusiasm for travel. Before putting a visitor figure into the budget, ask how it becomes an overnight stay, what accommodation those visitors choose and why they would book your property. Those missing steps matter more to your sales plan than the size of the headline.

Preserve the unit being measured

HVS ANAROCK's May 2026 overview estimated approximately 4,548 million domestic tourist visits in calendar 2025. That is its reported visits estimate, not a count of hotel room nights. UN Tourism distinguishes overnight visitors from same-day visitors and also distinguishes residence from nationality. Keep the original unit, period and source visible whenever management uses tourism statistics. Do not relabel visits as unique travellers or paid hotel stays.

Start with guests you can identify

Ask sales to divide attainable demand into relevant purposes: corporate work, project stays, leisure, events, medical travel or pilgrimage where appropriate. Do not assume every category exists locally. For each, identify the customer, booking decision-maker, timing, expected stay and alternatives. Use booking records and actual conversations. A list of nearby attractions is not a demand forecast unless management can explain who visits, when they need accommodation and why your offer fits.

Build an evidence ledger

Separate recurring business, confirmed future bookings, active enquiries and untested opportunities. Record the basis for every material forecast assumption. An enquiry that did not convert may reveal a product gap, but it is not automatically next year's revenue. Where guest purpose has not been recorded reliably, acknowledge the missing information and improve collection. Do not retrospectively fill the database with assumptions simply to make a forecast look complete.

Look at dates and inventory consumption

Build the plan by month and relevant demand periods, not only an annual occupancy percentage. Ask whether potential customers travel when you have rooms available. Review length of stay, room sharing, group requirements and cancellation patterns from your own records. Guest numbers and room requirements are different. The same sales opportunity can use inventory differently depending on the booking arrangement, so the forecast needs to reach room nights before it becomes operationally useful.

Forecast execution, not just opportunity

For each new segment, identify what the team must do to win it: approach accounts, improve response times, change an offer or fix a product limitation. Budget the work and name the person responsible. Keep confirmed business distinct from expected renewals and speculative growth. Your owner review should ask whether commercial activity is progressing against the plan, rather than waiting until month-end to discover that broad market growth did not produce bookings.

Keep promotional traffic separate from demand

An increase in website visits, social engagement or destination searches may indicate interest, but your budget still needs bookings. Ask marketing to connect campaign activity with enquiries and sales using the evidence available. Do not claim perfect attribution where several touchpoints are involved. Keep the cost of generating an opportunity visible. A growing audience can be useful without being sufficient evidence to add rooms sold to next month's forecast or approve a permanent increase in operating expenditure.

Check whether the product fits

A segment may be active locally and still be unsuitable for the property. Review room configuration, meal requirements, access, expected arrival times and price expectations before building it into the plan. If changes are needed, estimate their cost and timing. Avoid announcing a new sales focus while leaving operations unable to serve it. The owner should see whether the opportunity fits the existing hotel, requires a modest adaptation or depends on a more substantial repositioning.

Review lost business without exaggeration

Ask the team to record why relevant enquiries fail, using the customer's explanation where available. A lost enquiry can reveal availability constraints, poor response or a missing facility. It does not automatically prove that adding the facility would have secured the sale. Follow up enough cases to identify a pattern, then test a proportionate response. Do not justify major expenditure by adding together every unconverted enquiry as though all would become guaranteed future room nights.

Update the forecast when evidence changes

Set a routine for replacing assumptions with actual bookings and revising opportunities that no longer look credible. Keep the original forecast so the team can learn where its judgement was strong or weak. The purpose is not to punish every difference; forecasts involve uncertainty. The owner should distinguish a reasonable assumption overtaken by events from a number that never had a defensible basis. Better forecasting comes from that distinction and from visible commercial follow-through.

A practical owner scenario — hypothetical

Imagine the sales forecast includes a large increase attributed to domestic tourism. Ask the team to identify the customers and dates behind it. If the explanation remains a national percentage, move that increase out of secured demand and into an opportunity requiring work. Assign the relevant sales activity and a review point. If enquiries subsequently convert, update the forecast from actual evidence. This hypothetical case does not imply the opportunity is absent. It shows why an owner needs visibility into the difference between a favourable market story, a commercial assumption and rooms already booked.

What to put in front of the owner

Request the segment-level booking report, enquiry log, confirmed business register, cancellation records and monthly sales action plan. Use a defined review period and make sure the GM and finance team agree which version is current. Where the information is missing, record that limitation rather than filling the gap with an estimate presented as fact. Keep the main review short, with supporting detail available for questions. The purpose is not to create paperwork for its own sake: it is to give the owner enough evidence to approve a decision, understand the exposure and hold the responsible manager accountable for completion.

Questions for the next owner–GM meeting

Start with these questions: Which room nights are supported by evidence? What is still an assumption? What must sales or operations do to make the opportunity attainable? Ask management to distinguish an observed result, its explanation and the proposed response. They are not the same thing. Where the answer involves judgement, identify the assumption and what evidence could change it. Agree which decisions belong to the GM within the approved plan and which need owner approval. Avoid taking over routine execution; require clarity about responsibility, timing and the next update instead. The central risk to guard against is budgeting national visitor growth as though it were your own secured room-night growth.

The immediate owner action

For the next review cycle, rebuild the next quarter's forecast from identifiable demand segments and label its unsupported assumptions. Give the task a named lead and a completion date appropriate to your hotel. Check whether it was completed and what changed, rather than simply carrying the same instruction forward each month. Record any financial commitment separately from a discussion or recommendation. If the evidence does not support the original response, revise it openly. The owner-side objective is a better operating decision with a clear basis, not a more optimistic presentation or a promise that cannot be verified.
