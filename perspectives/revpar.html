Performance

RevPAR is not the only metric your hotel needs. Here is what to track alongside it.

Veritas Perspectives · Published 2 October 2026 · Updated 25 October 2026

← Back to Perspectives

Keep the metric and correct the expectation

RevPAR is useful. It becomes misleading when it is treated as a complete answer to whether your hotel is working for you. A stronger owner approach is not to discard the measure but to place it beside acquisition cost, operating contribution, cash and the condition of the asset. Ask which question a number answers before allowing it to determine management priorities or rewards.

What room-revenue performance can establish

RevPAR expresses room revenue relative to available room inventory. HVS ANAROCK's May 2026 overview reports occupancy, average room rate and RevPAR together for calendar 2025, including a nationwide RevPAR range of approximately ₹5,400–₹5,600. The report's use of those measures provides market context. It does not establish your property's cost base, cash conversion or maintenance requirement, which need separate records. [web:17]

Compare like with like

Keep reporting periods, inventory treatment and revenue definitions consistent. Note rooms unavailable for sale and other changes that could affect interpretation. Do not judge your GM against a national range without explaining why that comparison is relevant to your property. Guest mix, location, facilities and commercial structure matter. A comparison should prompt a useful investigation, not become a verdict detached from the business being measured.

Add acquisition and whole-hotel economics

Ask what the hotel spent to generate the room revenue and define a retained-revenue view. Then review material food, beverage, event and other departmental activity. A property with substantial non-room operations needs to know whether those activities contribute appropriately. More total revenue is not automatically a better result either. The purpose is to connect the rooms business with the wider hotel rather than replace one incomplete headline with another.

Continue through profit and cash

Review a consistently defined operating-profit measure and then reconcile it with cash movements and owner obligations. Show collections, supplier timing, financing payments and essential expenditure separately. Do not imply that a per-available-room profit figure is the same as money available for withdrawal. Your accounts should explain the connections between the measures while preserving their distinct meanings. If management cannot reconcile them, simplify and resolve the definitions first.

Include the building and incentives

Discuss necessary maintenance and refurbishment alongside operating performance. Ask whether the hotel is achieving results while preserving its intended service and condition. Then review incentives: a target dominated by one revenue measure may leave other responsibilities underemphasised. This is a governance question, not an accusation. Choose a short balanced set linked to the owner's objectives and ensure the GM understands how trade-offs will be judged.

Avoid swapping one slogan for another

It is tempting to replace RevPAR with a new favourite measure and declare the problem solved. Resist that shortcut. Contribution, operating profit and cash each answer different questions and can each be misinterpreted. Use the smallest set that covers the decisions you need to make and agree its definitions. The owner should be able to explain why a measure is present. If a number adds complexity without changing a decision, move the detail out of the main dashboard.

Separate operational and financing responsibilities

A GM may influence revenue and operating costs while financing structure and owner withdrawals sit elsewhere. Keep those responsibilities visible when reviewing outcomes. This does not excuse poor cash communication: management still needs to forecast material obligations and raise issues early. It does prevent an unfair performance discussion that attributes every financing consequence to hotel operations or every operating weakness to ownership. A clear bridge allows both sides to act on the decisions they actually control.

Review targets before rewarding the result

Ask whether the chosen performance measures remain consistent with the agreed product and service level. If a target is achieved while complaints, overdue work or collections worsen, investigate the trade-off. Do not presume deliberate gaming, but do not ignore the pattern. Incentives should be understandable and documented with appropriate professional input. The owner and GM need a shared view of what constitutes success before the period begins, not a different interpretation after the numbers arrive.

Use the metric as an invitation to investigate

A weaker RevPAR result can prompt questions about price, demand, inventory and customer mix. It does not identify the cause by itself. A stronger result deserves the same scrutiny: determine whether it reflects durable commercial progress or unusual dates. Link the investigation to records and customer evidence. Your objective is not to defend a metric, but to understand the business well enough to choose the next operating action and assess its consequences.

A practical owner scenario — hypothetical

Imagine RevPAR improves after a change in customer mix, while operating cash weakens. The figures are not necessarily contradictory. Acquisition expense, collections, supplier timing or necessary work may explain part of the gap. Ask management to reconcile the movements instead of selecting the measure that supports its preferred story. This hypothetical example does not make RevPAR useless. It shows why room-revenue efficiency is one stage in understanding your business and why the owner needs the connection through costs, profit, working capital and commitments before deciding what the result means.

What to put in front of the owner

Request the room-revenue calculation, available-inventory record, acquisition-cost report, departmental results, cash bridge and asset plan. Use a defined review period and make sure the GM and finance team agree which version is current. Where the information is missing, record that limitation rather than filling the gap with an estimate presented as fact. Keep the main review short, with supporting detail available for questions. The purpose is not to create paperwork for its own sake: it is to give the owner enough evidence to approve a decision, understand the exposure and hold the responsible manager accountable for completion.

Questions for the next owner–GM meeting

Start with these questions: What does this metric answer? What does it leave out? Could the target improve while the owner's actual position worsens? Ask management to distinguish an observed result, its explanation and the proposed response. They are not the same thing. Where the answer involves judgement, identify the assumption and what evidence could change it. Agree which decisions belong to the GM within the approved plan and which need owner approval. Avoid taking over routine execution; require clarity about responsibility, timing and the next update instead. The central risk to guard against is rewarding a single top-line improvement while missing cost, collection or condition problems.

The immediate owner action

For the next review cycle, retain RevPAR in the dashboard but connect it to contribution, cash and essential asset expenditure. Give the task a named lead and a completion date appropriate to your hotel. Check whether it was completed and what changed, rather than simply carrying the same instruction forward each month. Record any financial commitment separately from a discussion or recommendation. If the evidence does not support the original response, revise it openly. The owner-side objective is a better operating decision with a clear basis, not a more optimistic presentation or a promise that cannot be verified.
