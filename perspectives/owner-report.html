Reporting

The seven numbers every hotel owner and GM should review each month

Veritas Perspectives · Published 2 October 2026 · Updated 25 October 2026

← Back to Perspectives

Build a review, not another report

Your monthly meeting should explain whether the hotel is trading well, collecting money and protecting its ability to operate. A long profit-and-loss statement can contain the answers without making them understandable. The seven measures below are a proposed owner dashboard, not a universal reporting standard. Agree the definitions with your GM and accountant, reconcile them and use the meeting to make decisions rather than merely acknowledge the figures.

1. Retained room revenue

Review room revenue after the acquisition costs included in your agreed management definition. State those costs explicitly. Examine the number alongside rooms sold and achieved rates so management can explain the movement. Avoid an undefined net-rate figure that changes its boundary between months. The question is whether commercial activity produces more retained value, not simply more reservations. HVS ANAROCK's market occupancy, rate and RevPAR figures provide context, not a substitute for this property-level analysis.

2. Departmental contribution and 3. gross operating profit

Review the contribution of rooms, food and beverage, events and other material activities using consistent cost boundaries. Then examine gross operating profit under the hotel's agreed accounting structure. Ask what changed because of demand and what changed because of operational delivery. Keep contractual fees and other subsequent obligations visible. Do not let a strong department's result conceal a separate problem or describe operating profit as cash available for the owner.

4. Operating cash conversion and 5. overdue receivables

Reconcile reported performance with cash generated. Identify timing differences and working-capital movements rather than judging every bank movement as operating success or failure. Show overdue balances by account and agreed payment terms. Separate disputed invoices from routine delays. Sales and finance should review important exposures together: a celebrated account is not a complete success while its unpaid invoices remain unresolved. Name the next collection action and the person responsible.

6. Committed cash obligations

Show material payments due over the practical planning horizon, including debt service, approved capital expenditure and other commitments appropriate to your structure. Distinguish contracted payments from discretionary proposals. Compare the calendar with available funds and realistic collections. Annual profitability does not answer whether a particular month needs funding. Your owner review should identify pressure early enough to consider options rather than receive a request for an emergency transfer.

7. Asset-condition exposure

Use an engineering-supported schedule of overdue critical work, unavailable rooms and associated spending. Separate essential operation and safety from protection of the existing product and discretionary enhancement. Do not describe every attractive improvement as urgent maintenance, but do not allow current profit to rely on ignoring necessary work. Your accountant and engineering team should help distinguish operating expenditure, capital expenditure and the cash needed for each.

Make the seven numbers work together

Every measure needs a definition, data owner, comparison and explanation of material movement. Agree which exceptions require immediate contact and which can wait for the monthly meeting. Add detail when it changes a decision, not because software can produce another chart. The GM should arrive with causes, options and recommendations. Your role is to challenge assumptions, approve reserved decisions and ensure that agreed actions are completed.

Use comparisons with a clear purpose

Show actual results against the approved plan and a relevant prior period, explaining unusual timing or operating changes. Avoid comparing unlike months without context. The dashboard should make questions easier, not imply certainty from a coloured indicator. When a measure changes definition, reconcile the old and new basis before presenting a trend. The owner should know whether apparent improvement reflects performance or merely a change in how the hotel counted it.

Separate reporting from forecasting

The monthly dashboard records what happened; the cash and operating forecast describes what management expects next. Keep them linked but distinct. Ask how the actual result changes the forward plan and which assumptions now need revision. Do not rewrite the original budget so differences disappear. Retain a clear approved baseline and document changes. This allows the owner and GM to learn whether the business changed or the original planning judgement was weak.

Make ownership of information clear

Finance may compile the dashboard, but sales, operations and engineering must explain their parts. Agree submission dates and who signs off material information. Avoid a reporting process in which the GM sees unresolved differences for the first time during the owner meeting. The owner should expect coordinated information without demanding that every department create separate competing packs. One reconciled account of the business is preferable to several polished but incompatible presentations.

Keep the meeting focused on exceptions and decisions

Circulate the report early enough to review it. Use meeting time for causes, options and approvals rather than reading each line aloud. Record the decision, responsible person and completion date, and start the next meeting with unresolved actions. A dashboard succeeds when it changes the quality and timing of management decisions. If it repeatedly produces discussion without action, simplify the meeting and clarify authority before adding another metric or software subscription.

A practical owner scenario — hypothetical

Imagine the dashboard shows stronger operating profit while overdue receivables and critical repair work both rise. The meeting should not end with congratulations or a demand to cut costs further. Ask what created each movement and how the forward cash plan changes. There may be legitimate timing explanations, but they need evidence. This hypothetical example shows why the seven measures should be read together. A dashboard is valuable when it reveals the relationship between performance, collection and obligations early enough for the owner and GM to agree a practical response.

What to put in front of the owner

Request the agreed dashboard definitions, reconciled accounts, cash forecast, aged receivables, payment calendar and engineering backlog. Use a defined review period and make sure the GM and finance team agree which version is current. Where the information is missing, record that limitation rather than filling the gap with an estimate presented as fact. Keep the main review short, with supporting detail available for questions. The purpose is not to create paperwork for its own sake: it is to give the owner enough evidence to approve a decision, understand the exposure and hold the responsible manager accountable for completion.

Questions for the next owner–GM meeting

Start with these questions: Which number changed materially? Can we reconcile it? What decision does it require and who is responsible? Ask management to distinguish an observed result, its explanation and the proposed response. They are not the same thing. Where the answer involves judgement, identify the assumption and what evidence could change it. Agree which decisions belong to the GM within the approved plan and which need owner approval. Avoid taking over routine execution; require clarity about responsibility, timing and the next update instead. The central risk to guard against is mistaking an attractive sales or operating-profit result for available owner cash.

The immediate owner action

For the next review cycle, agree a one-page dashboard and trial it for the next reporting cycle before adding more measures. Give the task a named lead and a completion date appropriate to your hotel. Check whether it was completed and what changed, rather than simply carrying the same instruction forward each month. Record any financial commitment separately from a discussion or recommendation. If the evidence does not support the original response, revise it openly. The owner-side objective is a better operating decision with a clear basis, not a more optimistic presentation or a promise that cannot be verified.
